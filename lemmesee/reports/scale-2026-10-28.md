# Twice the databases, 28 October 2026

Round 1f, as [its description](../round-1f/README.md) sets it, built and run
by the agent the same day as round 1e: round 1e's look taught on 81
databases instead of 41, 42 more taken from BIRD and loaded to PostgreSQL,
to see whether the count of databases is what limits it. Measured at
research commit `5b679a0` with rounds 1e's and 1f's files uncommitted; raw
output and every judge's answers in
[data/scale-2026-10-28/](data/scale-2026-10-28/).

## The system in one paragraph

`dashbin see` suggests the first five dashboard panels for a PostgreSQL or
MySQL database it has never seen, with no language model in production. A
generator writes candidate questions from the schema and the planner's
statistics — a base table, a measure (rows, a sum, a product of two
columns), a time and a breakdown, through many-to-one joins — compiled to
SQL; a database here has 151 of them in the median and 13 thousand at most.
A ranker of 56 KB, boosted trees taught by what Gemini and Claude Haiku ask
first about 480 schemas (round 1c), orders them. The **look** (rounds
1d–1f) runs the thirty best on the database and reads each result as
numbers — rows, the share one value holds, blanks, a time's coverage,
repeated labels, codes as labels — and a model of 8.8 KB of what a judge
keeps reorders them; then the five are chosen greedily with penalties for
what two panels share, tuned on how many of the five the teachers keep.
Language models are only offline teachers and blind judges
([teaching.md](../teaching.md)): a judge sees a panel's title and its first
twelve rows, never its SQL or what chose it, and says whether it would keep
it on a first overview; the judge that taught a model never counts it.

## The databases

**BIRD** (bird-bench.github.io, CC BY-SA 4.0): of its 69 training and 11
development databases, 42 loaded, each from its SQLite file to PostgreSQL by
`round-1/fixtures/sqlite.py` — a column keeps its declared type only if
every value fits it, so BIRD's dates in text columns stay text; a primary
key when the rows keep it; foreign keys `NOT VALID` where the parent's key
exists and the types meet. Left out: 25 that CTU already serves under
another name or that are pilot fixtures (AdventureWorks as works_cycles,
Sakila as movie_3, `financial`, Stack Exchange's), found by their table
names; three made for learning; four of more than 4 GB. Loading all 42 took
thirteen minutes.

**Twelve held out**, drawn with seed 20261031 before any was loaded:
address, card_games, coinmarketcap, cookbook, human_resources, legislator,
menu, movie, soccer_2016, superstore, synthea, video_games. The other
thirty teach, with round 1d's 28 and round 1e's 23 (its ten held out are
counted already): **81 databases, 93 in all, 45 of them on PostgreSQL**.

Pools are round 1d's — the ranker's thirty best, round 1c's and round 1d's
fives, round 1's B1 five — 1 277 distinct panels, 388 on the twelve. One
statement reached round 1's limit of 5 seconds, on authors; two of
talkingdata's failed, because round 1c's compiler writes a flag kept as an
integer, 0 or 1, as a boolean, which MySQL accepts and PostgreSQL refuses —
a fault found here and not yet fixed.

an objective that counts whole panels kept over a few dozen databases.

## What the database pays

The look runs about thirty statements on a database where showing the five
would run five. Over all 93 databases, 2 632 statements: 229 ms in the
median and 1 021 ms at the 95th percentile as the client measured them,
each including about 185 ms of starting the client in its container, so
about 45 ms and 0.8 s of the database's own; per database 6.8 s in the
median, 30 s at the 95th percentile, 2 minutes at most, where SalesDB's
statements on 6.7 million rows reached the limit. 17 of 2 632 reached it,
and two failed as above.

## What it means, and what does not follow

- **What is clear: the look beats the rules.** Round 1f keeps more of its
  five than round 1 and round 1c by 0.4 to 1.2 panels on the twelve and 0.2
  to 0.5 on the 28, by every judge that taught nothing; five of those eight
  differences are clear of chance.
- **The count of databases stopped being the limit near forty.** Doubling
  from 41 to 81 moved the twelve by 0.42 by Sonnet and not at all by Gemini,
  and the 28 not at all; the curve is flat past forty for this look, these
  numbers and these teachers.
- **The tuned choice is noise at this size.** One teacher's labels on two
  databases of 81 swung its penalties from heavy to none and the 28's count
  by Sonnet by 0.3. Differences between round 1d, 1e and 1f of that size —
  round 1e's own over round 1d included — are not settled by these counts.
- **What is left is the order within a database.** The candidates hold four
  to five kept panels of five; the look takes three and a half. The look is
  taught panel by panel on a mean of three teachers who agree with the
  counting judges on three panels in four; nothing is taught what the five
  as a whole should be.
- **The twelve are few and BIRD's.** Each count over twelve databases moves
  by a whole panel in its interval; the judges agree more on BIRD's
  databases than on CTU's, and every judge is a model.

## What follows

1. **Teach the order, not the panel, and stop tuning on noise**: a ranking
   objective within each database instead of a probability per panel; a
   larger look now that 2 600 panels teach it; penalties fixed, or tuned
   with an objective that does not flip, and every count averaged over
   several seeds; a teacher of the counting judges' camp, Gemini, with
   Sonnet and the cheap three left to count — each chosen by folds over the
   81, the twelve counted once.
2. Fix the compiler's integer flag on PostgreSQL.
3. Carry the question, the ranker, the penalties and the look into Rust,
   and decide how few statements the look may run.
4. Round 2's judged set, with people.

## Reproducing

```bash
sh corpus/see-bird.sh <bird database>...
round-1b/.venv/Scripts/python -I round-1f/look.py pool corpus/round-1c/learn corpus/round-1d corpus/see corpus/round-1f/look <fixture-engine>...
round-1b/.venv/Scripts/python -I round-1f/look.py pack corpus/round-1f/look <the twelve>
round-1b/.venv/Scripts/python -I round-1f/look.py pack corpus/round-1f/look <the thirty>
python -I round-1d/gemini.py cut corpus/round-1f/look
JUDGE_KEY=<key> python -I round-1d/jev.py ask corpus/round-1f/look 64
JUDGE_KEY=<key> python -I round-1d/gemini.py ask corpus/round-1f/look <endpoint> <model> <judge> 42
JUDGE_KEY=<key> python -I round-1d/gemini.py ask corpus/round-1f/look <endpoint> <model> gemini 8 --packets=1,2,3
python -I round-1d/gemini.py gather corpus/round-1f/look <judge>
round-1b/.venv/Scripts/python -I round-1f/look.py evaluate corpus/round-1d/look corpus/round-1e/look corpus/round-1f/look corpus/see corpus/round-1d
```

`corpus/see-bird.sh` is local: for each database it runs
`round-1/fixtures/bird.sh`, which unpacks BIRD's archive once, checks its
hash in `round-1/fixtures/hashes.txt` and loads the SQLite file to
PostgreSQL, then round 1's `see.sh`, with a line on what the database holds.
Round 1c's `pack.py` now clamps a threshold LightGBM writes as 1e300 to
float32's largest, which changes no score. Keys were read from where they
are kept into the environment of the one command that used them, never
printed or written. Measured on Windows 11 with Python 3.13, LightGBM
4.7.0, PostgreSQL 18.6 and MySQL 8.4.13 in Docker.
