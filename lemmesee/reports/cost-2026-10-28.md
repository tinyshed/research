Round 1i, as [its description](../round-1i/README.md) sets it, built and run
by the agent the same day as round 1h. Every pool statement of rounds
1d–1f, 2 632 on 93 databases, was explained, run whole and run on samples
of its base table, and timed. Every five is counted with the answers the
judges that never teach had already given panel by panel, on the 50
databases they answered. No language model was asked anything. Measured
at research commit `b6e9699` with this round's files uncommitted; the
measures are in [data/cost-2026-10-28/](data/cost-2026-10-28/).

## What was run

Each pool statement, as rounds 1d–1f ran it, was asked of its database
three ways in one session per database: its plan, without running it;
whole, with a limit of 60 s; and, where its base table holds 10 000 rows
or more, on samples of 10 % and 1 %, counts and sums scaled back. On
PostgreSQL the sample is `TABLESAMPLE SYSTEM`, whole pages chosen at
random, repeatable. MySQL has no sampling clause, so the sample is twenty
runs of the table's primary key, each from a random starting value, as
many rows each as make up the share. A table without an integer first key
column is not sampled; sixteen MySQL databases have one of 10 000 rows or
more. Of 2 632 statements, two ran out of time whole, both Credit's, and
two failed as in round 1f, on talkingdata's flag.

The look scoring a sampled result is round 1g's baseline, the small look
on round 1e's numbers. It is taught on full results by family folds, five
folds by three draws, exactly as round 1g taught it, and the five is
chosen with round 1g's each-axis-once rule. So here the five kept by
whole reading is 2.88 by Opus, 2.76 by Sonnet and 2.96 by Gemini, panel
by panel; under that rule these are lower than round 1g's own per-panel
counts, which were taken without the rule.

The judges answered on full results. A sampled panel is counted as the
full one was: what a sample changes here is what the look chooses, and
that is what is counted. A 100 000-row sample is at least as large as a 1 %
one on every table under ten million rows, so the 93 databases' answer
holds for it.

Times are the client's, over a local connection to round 1's two servers
in Docker, with caches warm from round 1f. Round 1f's median of 6.8 s per
database was mostly the cost of starting a client for each statement; the
database's own share is 0.22 s. The copy with 44 million rows is larger
than the server's memory, so it was read partly from disk. A production
database's cold cache would cost more, and the limit is what bounds it.

## What follows

The look a program can ship:

1. read round 1c's best ten questions, not thirty;
2. read a table over 100 000 rows on a sample of about 100 000 rows: on
   PostgreSQL by pages, on MySQL by runs of an integer key;
3. stop any statement at half a second, and read it as not run;
4. choose the five with each axis once.

The database pays at most ten half-seconds, and 0.05 s on the median
database here. The rows of a joined table are the one cost a sample of
the base table does not bound, and the limit catches them.

The plan's third resource is untouched: saying when a guess is a guess,
and keeping a meaning a person confirmed. With what the five is worth near
its teachers and its price now bounded, that is what is left.
