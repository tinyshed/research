# What looking costs a store, 30 October 2026

Chapter 14 bounds what `see` may cost a source before it is built: *its
catalog and statistics, and at most 10 000 rows a table, inside 2 s a table —
never a full scan*. The prototype in `see-prototype/` read as lemmesee's
rounds read, whole tables and all. This round holds it to the bound, and
measures both on every PostgreSQL store of lemmesee's corpus with a table
above 10 000 rows: 36 stores, from 11 864 rows to 32 million in their biggest
table, each looked at unbounded and bounded, one after the other, twice.

| Question | Answer | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| Did `see` keep to the bound before? | No. 35 of the 36 stores had a big table read whole, 85 times in all; one statement read 32.5 million rows; one table took 48 s of the server | the bound needed code, not care |
| Does it now? | On all 36, both passes: no big table read whole, at most 9 000 rows of a table in a statement, at most 82 ms of the server on a table | the bound holds on this corpus |
| What did it save the server? | 124.8 s and 124.2 s of server time over the 36 looks became 1.36 s and 1.35 s | |
| And the person waiting? | The looks took 260.6 s and 257.0 s, now 147.2 s and 146.9 s; on the biggest store 42.2 s became 3.2 s. What is left is `docker exec` started for every statement, about 0.2 s each | one connection for a look, not a process a statement |
| How far are its answers from the whole's? | Of the numbers it gave, 81 % within 1 % and 98 % within 25 %; of its charts by day, 84 % within 10 % at the median day; of its rankings, the same first five in 68 % | estimates are marked `≈`, with what they are |
| What did it give up? | 114 of its 835 statements, refused with their reason: a quarter of the panels and a third of the numbers it would have drawn | distinct counts and logs dense beyond 7 000 rows a week are the next work |
| Does it hold on a production store? | On two of the owner's, PostgreSQL 16 and 18 over ssh, read only: 346 ms and 302 ms of server time, no error, every panel of the smaller and all five of the bigger's screen drawn | the bound ships with `see` |

## In plain words

Before, to draw "orders by day" `see` asked the database to count every
order, and the database read the whole table to do it: a census, every
person counted, up to 32 million rows for one chart. Right, and on someone
else's production database not allowed.

Now, before every question, `see` looks for a cheaper way to the answer, in
this order, and takes the first that holds:

1. **The table is small** (10 000 rows or fewer): read it, it costs nothing.
2. **The database already knows.** It keeps notes on every table — about how
   many rows, how many different values a column has, which values are most
   common and how often — and refreshes them itself. Reading the notes reads
   no row.
3. **An index on the time, and few rows in the window**: like a book's index,
   it opens only the pages the answer is on.
4. **The table is a log**, new rows always written at its end: the newest
   rows are on its last pages, and only those are read.
5. **Otherwise a poll instead of a census**: about 7 000 rows taken from
   pages across the table, counted, and scaled up to the whole. The answer is
   marked `≈`.
6. **When a poll cannot answer honestly** — how many *different* people,
   who did it twice or more — nothing is drawn, and the panel says why. An
   empty panel is better than a wrong one.

Over all of it stands **a guard inside the database**. Before a question
runs, the database is asked how it means to answer it; if its plan reads a
big table whole, the question is refused, however the rules above got it
wrong.

So the server reads at most 9 000 rows a question instead of millions, and
130 s of its work became 1.4 s. The look itself is not quite twice as fast,
because most of what is left is starting `docker exec` for each question —
a fifth of a second each time, nothing to do with the database.

## Environment and reproduction

- `see-prototype/` at 86fedb5: `src/core/bound.rs` plans a statement,
  `src/core/run.rs` runs it; `--unbounded` is the old reading, in the same
  binary. `looks.py` runs both on every store, `cost.py` measures a look,
  `drift.py` compares answers. Built with rustc 1.97.1, release.
- The 36 stores: every PostgreSQL snapshot of `lemmesee/corpus/see` with a
  table above 10 000 rows by its catalog's estimate — 30 public databases
  (BIRD's, the airlines demo) and six of round 1m's bench — restored in
  round 1's container, PostgreSQL 18.6, `shared_buffers` 128 MB, Docker 29.6.2
  on WSL2, AMD Ryzen 7 7700 (8 cores), 32 GB, Windows 11 Pro 26300.
- Two passes, 2026-10-30 15:05:54Z to 15:32:58Z; in each, store after store,
  the unbounded look (A) then the bounded (B). Nothing was built or run
  meanwhile.
- A look is `see <snapshot.json> --docker lemmesee-round-1-postgres-1 --user
  lemmesee --doc <doc.json> --doc-only [--unbounded]` with `SEE_TRACE` naming
  its trace; its cost is every traced statement run again, with the settings
  it ran under, as `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)`: the rows each scan node
  returned and threw away times its loops, a table read whole where a scan is
  in sequence or along an index with nothing to look up, and the plan's own
  planning and execution time.

```
python looks.py <see.exe> out/round --passes 2 > out/round.jsonl
python drift.py out/round/1-a out/round/1-b
```

Raw output in [data/see-bound-2026-10-30](data/see-bound-2026-10-30):

once for the window's end and once for its rows, up to 18 000 rows.

## How the bounded look answered

| How | Statements |
|---|---|
| as written: every table it names is small | 296 |
| over a sample of the big table's pages, or a sample's window end | 220 |
| refused before it ran | 114 |
| the statistics' most common values | 95 |
| the newest rows of a log, or its newest pages for a window's end | 75 |
| the catalog's row estimate | 19 |
| the statistics' distinct values | 10 |
| exact through an index | 5 |
| an empty window, its table's last row before it | 1 |

Five decisions carried the bound, each found when a look broke it:

- **A sample aims below the line.** Pages come whole, so a sample of a
  table is a little more or less than it aims at: 8 973 rows for 8 000 on
  `lineitem`. It aims at 7 000, 5 500 when its rows each look a key up in a
  big table, and 800 for a window's end.
- **A window's end is read once a look.** The rounds' charts end where their
  table ends, `(SELECT MAX(t) FROM t)`, in every statement; each read the
  table again. Read once — through an index, off a log's newest pages, from a
  sample, or whole for a small table — and written into each statement as a
  literal, a table is read once a statement and every panel of a look ends on
  the same day.
- **Joins are lookups.** In the transaction: no sequential scan, no hash or
  merge join, no materialising, joins in their written order. A small table
  read whole then looks a big one up by its key, at most as many times as it
  has rows; a big table with no index on that key is refused.
- **The guard reads the plan in the database.** A `DO` block runs `EXPLAIN`
  on the statement and raises if it would scan a big table in sequence, or
  along an index with no condition to look up — which a planner told not to
  scan in sequence does instead. A window's end through an index is let pass:
  the guard sees no `Limit` above the scan, and calls one row off the end of
  the index a whole read.
- **A sample counts its own rows.** Every sampled statement carries a
  `COUNT(*)` of the rows behind each answer, taken off before it is shown:
  under 20 rows at the median day, 5 behind a value or 30 behind a number,
  the answer is not given.

## What it costs in answers

Every answer of the bounded look set against the unbounded look's, same pass
(the two passes agree to the digit: `REPEATABLE` samples the same pages).

| | Compared | Refused | Within 1 % | Within 10 % | Within 25 % |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| numbers | 172 | 54 | 81 % | 89 % | 98 % |

| | Compared | Median error at a day | Within 10 % | Median correlation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| charts by day | 133 | 0 | 84 % | 1.00 |

Of 304 rankings, the median shares all five of the whole's first five, and
68 % share every one. Of 613 panels the unbounded look drew, the bounded
refused 154.

The worst answers are of two kinds:

- **A count of distinct values from the statistics runs low.** `n_distinct`
  comes from `ANALYZE`'s sample of 30 000 rows and underestimates big skewed
  columns: 65 % low on bird_talkingdata's "sample_submission with events",
  23 % on bird_coinmarketcap's "coins with historical", 19 % on
  bird_olympics' "person with games", 12 % on airlines' "flights with
  boarding_passes". They are marked, but a mark is not enough for a number
  that far off.
- **A number over the last days of a log, from a sample of its pages,** is
  15–22 % off on round 1m's bench ("rentals, 30 days", "BOOKING done,
  7 days", "LINEITEM.L_EXTENDEDPRICE, 30 days", "payment_transactions.amount,
  30 days"): the window's rows are on a few pages, which a sample takes or
  leaves whole.

Rankings by a key of many near-equal values (the twelve flights with most
boarding passes) share none of the whole's first five; the whole's own first
five are a draw among ties.

## What it refuses

| Refused | Panels | Numbers |
|---|---|---|
| a count of distinct values over more rows than may be read | 79 | 10 |
| a sample too thin for a day | 29 | |
| a log's newest 7 000 rows hold under 7 days | 25 | |
| a sample with no row in its window | | 21 |
| a value's rows kept together on its pages | 14 | |
| a sample too thin for a value | 11 | |
| two or more | | 11 |

A count of distinct values is most of it: the people of a day, the
customers of a month. A sample cannot say how many different ones the whole
holds, and the bound reads too few rows to count them; only where an index
or a log's newest rows hold the whole window is it exact. Logs dense beyond
7 000 rows a week — airlines' bookings and boarding passes, over 10 000 a
day — cannot be drawn by day at all under the bound. The 21 numbers with no row in
their window are windows back from today over data that ended years ago:
the whole's answer is 0, which a sample cannot tell from a few, and only one
of those tables had its last row read exactly enough to say so.

## On a production store

Two of the owner's stores, PostgreSQL 16 and 18, read over ssh as before, in
a read-only session with half a second a statement, once each and bounded
only; only counts left the machine. The smaller's tables are all small: 54
statements as written, 302 ms of server time. The bigger's biggest table is
220 810 rows: 53 statements, 346 ms, none slower than 23 ms, no error; its
screen's five panels drawn, two exact through an index and three from the
newest rows of logs; of its 14 numbers, 4 estimated and 3 refused, distinct
counts and two or more.

Production showed two faults the corpus had not, both fixed before the
passes above: psql writes a line of context after the guard's refusal, and
the runner took that last line for an error, not a refusal; and a window's
end through an index was refused as a whole read, as the guard's bullet
says.

## What follows

- **One connection a look.** `docker exec` and `ssh` started for every
  statement are most of a bounded look's time.
- **Distinct counts under the bound**: exact over an index or a log's newest
  rows where they hold the window, as now; elsewhere an estimator that says
  its error, or nothing. And `n_distinct` of a whole column checked against
  its sample before it is believed.
- **A number over a log's last days from its newest rows**, when they hold
  the window, not from a sample of its pages.
- **MySQL**, which has no `TABLESAMPLE`: its estimates in
  `information_schema`, and samples by key range.
- Chapter 14's other measures, kept and harmful panels, are untouched by
  this round; a guessed panel the bound refuses is one fewer to judge.
